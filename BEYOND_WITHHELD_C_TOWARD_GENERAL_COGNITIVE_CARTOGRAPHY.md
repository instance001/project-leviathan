# Project Leviathan — Beyond Withheld C: Toward General Cognitive Cartography

## Status

Exploratory architecture / research direction.

This note extends the withheld-C method beyond abstract reasoning.

The central idea is:

> If withheld C provides a reliable way to plant verified landmarks in hidden cognitive territory, then the same surveying logic may eventually be adapted to other forms of cognition.

The immediate task is still narrow:

> First prove that withheld-C derivation can be measured reliably.

Everything beyond that must be earned experimentally.

---

## The primitive

The current Leviathan foothold is:

**A + B, with C withheld → attempt to derive C**

Where:

- A is grounded;
- B is grounded;
- the model is demonstrably competent in the relevant domain;
- C is absent from the controlled corpus;
- C is derived rather than supplied;
- C is independently verified.

A verified C becomes a landmark.

Repeated C-arrivals allow an operational topology of reachability to emerge.

This is the first measurement spine.

---

## The deeper possibility

If this primitive works, its importance may extend beyond abstract reasoning.

The real breakthrough would not be:

> "We mapped reasoning."

It would be:

> "We found a general method for establishing verified landmarks inside otherwise hidden cognitive territory."

Once one class of transition can be measured, other classes may become experimentally approachable.

The same basic pattern can be preserved:

**known cognitive territory**  
↓  
**withheld transformation or result**  
↓  
**attempted transition**  
↓  
**verification**  
↓  
**new landmark**  
↓  
**map outward**

---

## C does not have to mean deduction

In the base protocol, C is a novel conclusion derived from grounded premises.

Later, C could represent other kinds of cognitive transition.

Possible examples include:

### Analogy

A and B establish structures in one setting.

C is recognition of a valid structural correspondence elsewhere.

Question:

> How far can analogy travel before it becomes superficial resemblance?

### Generalization

A set of examples is supplied.

C is a rule or abstraction that was never stated directly.

Question:

> Which examples support stable generalization, and which produce brittle overreach?

### Causal inference

Several grounded events or mechanisms are known.

C is a previously unstated causal relation.

Question:

> Where does causal inference remain grounded, and where does post-hoc storytelling begin?

### Category formation

Several objects or relations are supplied.

C is a newly inferred category boundary or shared structure.

Question:

> Which features become salient enough to support a useful new grouping?

### Contradiction detection

A and B are individually grounded but jointly inconsistent.

C is recognition of the contradiction.

Question:

> How quickly and reliably does the system detect incompatible structure?

### Assumption revision

A prior working assumption is established.

New evidence B conflicts with it.

C is a revised model that better fits the evidence.

Question:

> What kinds of contradiction trigger productive reconsideration rather than defensive rationalization or collapse?

### Salience selection

A large amount of information is supplied.

C is identification of the subset most relevant to the task.

Question:

> What makes one relation cognitively "louder" than another?

### Missing-structure recognition

A and B suggest that an important relation is absent.

C may be:

> "There is a gap here, but it cannot yet be resolved."

This is not failure.

It may be a valid landmark in uncertainty topology.

### Intermediate-concept construction

A direct route from A to target D fails.

The system invents C as a useful intermediate abstraction.

Then:

**A + B → C**  
**C + additional structure → D**

Question:

> Can the system create new cognitive gearing rather than merely traverse existing paths?

## Multiple cognitive topologies

If these probes can be operationalized, "cognition" may not resolve into one map.

It may contain several interacting topologies.

Possible maps include:

- deductive reach;
- analogical reach;
- causal reach;
- abstraction;
- category formation;
- uncertainty handling;
- contradiction sensitivity;
- assumption revision;
- salience;
- memory accessibility;
- cross-domain transfer;
- cumulative novelty.

A system could be strong in one topology and weak in another.

For example:

> strong local deduction;  
> weak distant analogy;  
> strong causal chaining;  
> weak reconsideration;  
> broad cross-domain transfer;  
> sharp hallucination cliff.

This is more informative than a scalar intelligence score.

---

## Cognitive profiles instead of rankings

One long-term implication is that systems may be better described by topological profiles than by single performance numbers.

Two systems can achieve similar aggregate benchmark scores while being radically different cognitive animals.

One may:

- travel broadly but shallowly;
- travel narrowly but deeply;
- need frequent scaffolding;
- excel at cross-domain bridges;
- resist unsupported inference;
- generate many false bridges;
- recover well from contradiction;
- fail to notice contradiction at all.

The map describes shape.

The score merely compresses it.

---

## Why AI is a useful laboratory

Artificial systems offer experimental control that is difficult or impossible with humans.

We can:

- control corpora;
- vary curriculum order;
- duplicate architectures;
- create synthetic domains;
- reset conditions;
- hold token budgets constant;
- alter auxiliary-domain exposure;
- run thousands of matched trials;
- preserve every output and failure;
- compare raw and host-scaffolded behavior.

This makes AI a practical environment for developing the measurement machinery.

The goal is not to assume that results transfer directly to humans.

The goal is to develop testable methods.

---

## Possible human translation

If the method becomes robust in artificial systems, some probes may be adaptable to human participants.

The cleanest route may be controlled synthetic domains.

Example:

1. Teach participants a small invented universe.
2. Verify that its concepts and rules are learned.
3. Withhold C.
4. Present A and B.
5. Measure whether C is reached.
6. Record path, time, confidence, scaffolding, and failure mode.
7. Repeat from different starting points.
8. Introduce auxiliary conceptual tools.
9. Compare changes in reachability.

This avoids pretending that a person's lifetime knowledge can be fully controlled.

Instead, the experiment creates a new local cognitive territory whose contents are known.

---

## Human cognitive diversity

A useful human version should not collapse differences into "better" or "worse" cognition.

Different people may have different topological profiles.

One person may show:

- excellent spatial threadhopping;
- strong causal chaining;
- weak verbal analogy;
- slow initial bridging;
- long cumulative depth once grounded.

Another may show a completely different shape.

The interesting question becomes:

> What forms of learned structure make particular kinds of thought easier for this cognitive system?

That is potentially much more informative than ranking people on one general scale.

---

## Expertise as topology

Expertise may be describable partly as local topological richness.

An experienced mechanic may infer a fault from sparse evidence that means almost nothing to a layperson.

That need not imply radically superior abstract reasoning.

The expert may possess:

- more landmarks;
- denser local connections;
- cheaper bridges;
- better intermediate representations;
- stronger causal chains;
- more reliable failure recognition.

The local terrain is different.

This suggests a possible interpretation:

> Expertise may change not only what is known, but how easily useful cognitive routes can be traversed.

---

## Education and curriculum

The cross-domain work raises a larger educational question:

> Which learned structures make other learning easier?

If certain domains provide reusable relational machinery, curriculum design may be able to improve future cognitive reach.

Examples might include:

- algebra improving scientific transformation;
- geometry improving spatial reasoning;
- statistics improving uncertainty handling;
- programming improving decomposition;
- causal modelling improving explanation;
- analogy training improving transfer.

These are hypotheses only.

The important point is that curriculum could eventually be evaluated not only by:

> What facts were learned?

but also by:

> What new territory became easier to reach because those structures were learned?

---

## Curriculum as terrain construction

This leads to a stronger interpretation of Relational Curriculum Geometry.

A curriculum may not merely deliver information.

It may construct traversable cognitive terrain.

The experimental chain becomes:

**teach or train structure X**  
↓  
**verify competence**  
↓  
**probe withheld transitions**  
↓  
**measure new reachability**  
↓  
**compare topology before and after**

If reliable differences appear, curriculum design becomes partly a problem of building useful cognitive connectivity.

---

## From reasoning capacity to reasoning-with

A major hypothesis emerging from this work is:

> Observed reasoning may depend not only on abstract reasoning machinery, but on the topology of the structures available to reason with.

This distinction matters.

A system may possess strong abstract capability yet still fail when:

- the relevant concepts are absent;
- representations are too sparse;
- domains do not mesh cleanly;
- intermediate structures are missing;
- the required bridge is too distant;
- uncertainty rises beyond a stable boundary.

Conversely, a well-shaped learned territory may make difficult derivations comparatively easy.

The question therefore shifts from:

> "How good is the reasoner?"

toward:

> "What can this reasoner reach from the structures currently available?"

---

## Generalized lighthouse model

The lighthouse metaphor extends naturally.

In the first experiment, C is the lighthouse.

Later, the lighthouse may be:

- a valid analogy;
- a newly formed category;
- a detected contradiction;
- a revised assumption;
- a useful intermediate abstraction;
- a recognized uncertainty boundary;
- a causal relation.

The common rule remains:

> No landmark is planted merely because it sounds plausible.

It must survive whatever verification is appropriate to that cognitive function.

The map grows only from earned footholds.

## Leviathan's role

Leviathan can become a general cognitive survey rig.

It can preserve:

- starting conditions;
- task type;
- known territory;
- candidate transitions;
- derivation paths;
- rejected alternatives;
- uncertainty;
- verification;
- scaffolding;
- bridge cost;
- failure classes;
- promoted landmarks;
- cumulative downstream effects.

Over time, this could support comparison across:

- models;
- corpora;
- curricula;
- domains;
- host architectures;
- cognitive task classes.

---

## Research restraint

The implications are potentially broad, but the correct order is narrow.

The program should proceed through earned claims:

1. Can withheld C be measured cleanly?
2. Can the result be replicated?
3. Can relational distance be manipulated?
4. Can bridge cost be measured?
5. Can cross-domain assistance be predicted and observed?
6. Can corpus geometry alter the measured map?
7. Can earned C support reliable cumulative propagation?
8. Can the same surveying logic be adapted to other cognitive transitions?
9. Do similar patterns appear in controlled human learning experiments?

Each step should stand before the next is claimed.

---

## Core implication

If the withheld-C method works as intended, its deepest contribution may not be a new AI benchmark.

It may provide a general experimental pattern for studying hidden cognitive structure:

> establish known territory, withhold a meaningful transition, observe whether the transition is reached, verify it, plant the result as a landmark, and map outward.

That would turn cognition from something discussed only in broad abstractions into something that can be surveyed locally, incrementally, and comparatively.

The first map may concern abstract reasoning.

It does not have to be the last.
